[Presenter A · about 1 minute 40 seconds] Perempatan Fatmawati is a junction under the JORR toll viaduct in South Jakarta, just 326 metres from RSUP Fatmawati, a national referral hospital. About 80% of the vehicles here are motorcycles. Look at the diagram: in moving traffic, at the top, a car can pull aside for an ambulance. But at a red light, at the bottom, motorcycles fill the motorcycle box and every gap between cars, so the ambulance is boxed in. Indonesian law lets ambulances ignore signals, but a packed queue has nowhere to go. The only thing that clears the path is a green light for the ambulance's direction at the right moment. The junction is already at the worst level of service, with 85 seconds of delay per vehicle. And delay matters: survival from cardiac arrest drops about two percentage points for every minute of delay. Only 9% of Jakarta's emergency patients arrive by ambulance, but they are often the most critical, and for them minutes decide survival. Slow, unpredictable trips may even be part of why so few people call one.

Why existing approaches fall short

Current methods were not built for motorcycle traffic

What exists

  • Fixed-time and max-pressure plans ignore ambulances entirely
  • Pre-emption studies and digital twins model cars, not motorcycle boxes
  • Deep reinforcement learning works in research but is hard to audit
  • Studies that include two-wheelers treat them only as counts

What our project adds

  • A real hospital junction with motorcycle boxes and the city's own signal plan
  • Drivers yield only where there is physical room
  • An open dataset of ambulance passages with checked labels
  • Interpretable control behind a safety shield, judged on fairness
Evidence from our twin: naive pre-emption (green as soon as an ambulance is near) is fastest for ambulances, but leaves one direction 32% worse than today, so it fails our fairness test.

[Presenter A · about 1 minute 20 seconds] So why hasn't this been solved? Standard signal plans, fixed-time or max-pressure, don't consider ambulances at all. Research on ambulance pre-emption and traffic digital twins exists, but it models car traffic, not junctions where most vehicles are motorcycles that fill a motorcycle box. Deep reinforcement learning is popular in research, but it is hard to audit, which matters for a safety system. And the few studies that include two-wheelers treat them only as counts. Our project adds a real hospital junction with its motorcycle boxes and the city's actual signal plan, drivers who yield only where there is room, an open dataset of ambulance passages, and interpretable controllers behind a safety shield. One result shows why fairness matters: the simplest fix, turning the light green as soon as an ambulance is near, is the fastest for ambulances, but it leaves one direction 32% worse than today. [Presenter B · about 1 minute] Ambulance trips can't be recorded safely on real roads, so we generated our data, but we built it from real inputs and calibrated it on real counts. The roads come from OpenStreetMap, the signal timings are Dishub's actual four-stage plan, the traffic comes from the 13 turning movements counted at this junction, and the vehicle mix is about 80% motorcycles. We then tuned the traffic volume until the simulated queues matched queue lengths published for this junction. The result is 108 hours of simulated traffic, stored as 9 CSV tables of sensor readings: 77,760 learning rows, one every five seconds, and 732 ambulance trips. One honest caveat: to reproduce the real queues, the twin needs only about a quarter of the real vehicle counts, because simulated motorcycles queue less densely than Jakarta's. So the twin copies the congestion, not the exact counts, and we use it to compare controllers, not to predict exact real delays.

[Presenter B · about 1 minute] We used four kinds of method, each answering one question. Descriptive analysis checks whether the twin is realistic. Predictive models are the core: a priority-aware teacher rule gives the ambulance's direction a green light just in time to clear the queue ahead of it, and otherwise serves the busiest direction. Five model families learn to copy that rule from the sensor data, and we test them on simulations they never saw. Gradient boosting copies it best, with a macro-F1 of 0.96, against 0.28 for the best simple baseline. Inferential statistics compare controllers fairly, because every controller faces exactly the same traffic. Finally, a decision rule chooses: a controller must pass safety, cost and fairness gates, and Nash bargaining picks the one that best balances everyone's gains. Deep dive · Learning

Five model families learned the teacher from sensor rows

  • Task: predict which of four stages the teacher requests, from 75 sensor features, every 5 s
  • Fair test: trained on seeds 1 and 2 (51,840 rows), tested on seed 3 (25,920 rows, 1,986 with an ambulance)
  • Gradient boosting is best overall and when an ambulance is present; the single decision tree is too coarse
  • Unseen conditions: macro-F1 0.91 on an evening peak it never saw, 0.89 on a demand level it never saw
  • Sanity check: with shuffled labels the score falls to 0.25, the level of guessing

Deep dive · Decision rule

Three gates, then Nash bargaining, fixed before any result

Gate 1 · Safety

Collisions per vehicle-km must not be significantly above today's plan.

Gate 2 · Cost

Everyone else's delay must stay within 10% of max-pressure, the best ambulance-blind rule.

Gate 3 · Fairness

No approach may be more than 20% worse than under today's plan.

Among the methods that pass

  • Keep the Pareto front: methods no other method beats on both ambulance delay and general delay
  • Pick the largest Nash product: every party's gain over its fallback, multiplied

N = (Atoday − A) × (1.10·Rmp − R) × Πa (1.20 − Ra)

A: ambulance delay · R: everyone else's delay index (today = 1) · Ra: the index of approach a

At low demand: for gradient boosting, for longest queue, for the priority teacher.

Why Nash? A product is zero as soon as one party gains nothing, so no direction can be sacrificed for the ambulance. The parties are the ambulance, general traffic and each of the four approaches.
How sure? The four seeds are resampled 1,000 times and the rule is rerun: the share of resamples a method wins is its stability.
6.2Methodology section 8; traffic_twin/fatmawati/kpi.py; Nash (1950)
The decision rule was fixed before we saw any results. First three gates: a method must not be significantly less safe than today, must not cost general traffic more than 10% over the best ambulance-blind rule, and must not leave any approach more than 20% worse than today. Among the methods that pass, we take the Pareto front and choose the largest Nash product. The product goes to zero as soon as one party gains nothing, which is why it protects every direction. We then bootstrap the seeds to see how stable the winner is.

Deep dive · Stress tests

In heavy traffic ambulances still gain, but fairness breaks

  • Why delays are so long: these runs hold the calibrated peak for 4.4 hours, so the north approach, already at capacity, never recovers
  • The priority teacher cuts ambulance delay by 59% at design demand and 38% at high demand, and leaves the fewest ambulances stranded (1% and 5%)
  • The price: every adaptive method leaves its worst approach 3.2 to 4.2 times worse than today
No method passes the fairness gate in heavy traffic. We report this as a limit of signal control alone, not as a result to tune away.

Design and high demand are stress tests: the peak is held for more than four hours, which is harsher than a real peak with shoulders. Deep dive · Two what-ifs

Better timing has room to give; fewer motorcycles is not easier

Hindsight ceiling. Replaying each passage and keeping the best switch time halves max-pressure's ambulance delay. The best switch is usually at detection; the teacher waits for the last safe moment, so it captures only 15% of that gain.
70% motorcycles. With more cars, queues are longer for the same vehicle count: ambulance delay roughly doubles for every method, and no method passes the gates.
8.4data/fatmawati_eval_hindsight (24 passages, low demand, seed 1); reports/a3/kpi_moto70 (low demand, seeds 1–4)
Two what-if experiments. Left: the hindsight ceiling. We fork the simulation at the moment an ambulance is detected and try every switch time, keeping the best. With perfect timing, max-pressure's ambulance delay on those passages falls from 159 to 82 seconds. Naive pre-emption captures almost all of that gain because it switches at once; the teacher captures only 15% because it waits. That points to our next step: switch earlier, but bounded. Right: with 70% motorcycles instead of 80%, the same vehicles take more road, queues are longer and ambulance delay about doubles.
Deep dive · Safety

The shield keeps every red under 252 s; forcing a green does not

  • Request-only controllers never hold an approach red for more than 252 s: the plant's maximum green and starvation rules apply to them
  • Naive pre-emption forces and holds a green, as real pre-emption does, and left an approach red for 362 s in light traffic and 565 s in heavy traffic
  • Collisions: the safety gate is relative to today's plan. Only learned logistic regression raised lane collisions significantly, and it was excluded
  • Reported, not hidden: vehicles overlap inside the junction about 180 times per 75-minute run, a known limit of the simulator with filtering motorcycles
8.5reports/a3/kpi/kpi_methods.csv (longest red over the four approaches, low demand); DECISIONS section 13.11
Safety has two parts. Starvation: how long can a direction be held at red? Every controller that only requests stages stays at or below 252 seconds, because the plant enforces maximum green. Naive pre-emption bypasses the plant and held a red for 362 seconds. Collisions: the gate compares each method with today's plan on the same traffic. Only the learned logistic regression was significantly worse, and it was dropped. We also report junction overlaps honestly, about 180 per run: they are a known weakness of the simulator when motorcycles filter.

Everything site-specific sits in one JSON file, which is what makes the transfer case on the next slide possible. The right-hand table shows what each step costs: the evaluation grid is the expensive part.

Could it move to Bundaran HI? The method yes, the numbers not yet

Uncalibrated demo. Real road geometry and buildings; assumed demand, mix and signals. Not evidence about the real junction.
  • What the map already shows: the ring has three entries (Thamrin, Imam Bonjol, Sudirman) and four exits; the west leg is exit-only
  • What is missing: no published counts, signal plan or queue lengths; the busway and the 16:00–21:00 movement ban are not modelled
  • What a transfer needs: a one-day survey, a stage model for the ring, then the same nine-step protocol

Stable up to 40% of an assumed 6,860 vehicles per hour; above that the Thamrin approach queues back about 900 m.

We tested how far the pipeline carries to a very different junction: Bundaran HI, a signalised roundabout in central Jakarta. The road network, the lanes and the buildings come straight from OpenStreetMap through the same tools. But demand, the vehicle mix and the signals are assumptions, so this view is a demonstration, not a result. The exercise told us what a real transfer needs: a one-day traffic survey, because nothing is published for this junction, and a stage model that can describe a ring. The site file for Bundaran HI stays incomplete on purpose until that survey exists.
